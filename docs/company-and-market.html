Alwayse Engineering and the market for lifecycle services

Research reviewed on 23 November 2026 for an independent candidate demonstration.

Alwayse already helps customers select, design, install and maintain material-handling equipment. The commercial question for this pilot is whether a customer would pay a recurring fee for a defined level of service at one transfer deck, and whether the supplier could deliver it profitably. The prototype lets a reviewer examine that question before anyone commits to a price or availability target. Alwayse's four existing services

The company and the partnership

UKRI identifies Aston University and Alwayse Engineering Limited as partners in Innovate UK project 10146336. Its abstract connects international growth with a shift from component supply towards engineered solutions, using market research and business-model innovation. UKRI lists a grant of £109,540.50 and a funded period of 1 October 2025 to 30 September 2027. The October 2026 vacancy describes an 18-month associate appointment. These dates refer to the grant and the advertised post respectively. The university vacancy describes the partner by sector and location. An indexed copy of the same vacancy also carries Alwayse's name and Birmingham B6 4NF postcode. UKRI project record · October vacancy copy · Employer listing indexed on Indeed

Alwayse Engineering Limited is a Birmingham manufacturer of lifting and handling equipment, incorporated in 1940. It makes ball transfer units in the UK and sells through an international distributor network. Lifco consolidated the company in its Transportation Products division in 2023. Its acquisition-period report gives useful historical scale, with approximately £5.6m turnover in 2022 and 41 employees reported in 2023. The public sources reviewed here do not establish a current headcount or the service share of revenue. Companies House · Company history and distribution · Lifco's 2023 report

What evidence a pilot would need

  1. Operations. Identify the deck or zone, required operating hours, workarounds and the person authorised to classify a stop. Compare operator logs with maintenance tickets to avoid counting an upstream delay against the deck.
  2. Commercial. Ask procurement and finance who owns the equipment, who pays for disruption and who can approve a recurring charge. Obtain the customer's current spend and willingness to pay for a defined improvement.
  3. Delivery. Review work orders with service engineers or the local integrator. Validate parts use, travel, technician time, exclusions and whether promised response windows can be met.
  4. Decision. Agree on the performance definition, observation period, dispute process and decision owner before testing an offer. If breakdowns are rare or easy to work around, the customer may be better served by its current arrangement.

Review coverage and limits

The review covered the company's public profile, catalogue indices, eight solution categories, eleven sector categories, both pages of the project archive and their fifteen listed case studies. It also covered selection, design, fitting, mechanical maintenance, FAQs, technical support, catalogue access, news on the four service lines, distributor information and published sales terms. The catalogue indices spanned 132 listings across eleven pages on the company's .com and .co.uk sites. Individual engineering specifications were sampled where a customer case referred to them. Private distributor material, signed service agreements, downtime logs, margins and willingness-to-pay research were outside the public evidence. A published fee, credit schedule or guaranteed availability level for the illustrated pilot was not established. The simulator's numbers are assumptions that a customer, a service engineer and a commercial owner could replace together.
