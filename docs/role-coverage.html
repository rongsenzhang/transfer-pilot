What the pilot demonstrates

A material-handling customer buys equipment once. A service provider takes on continuing work, cost and responsibility. This case asks which responsibilities a customer would pay to transfer, what the supplier could deliver, and what evidence both sides would need before signing.

This crosswalk follows pages 2–5 of Aston Business School’s eight-page Servitization and Service Innovation Lead – KTP Associate job description, reference 1155-26. A later public listing uses reference R260050 and retains the same six main areas of work. The case uses illustrative operating conditions and prices. Customer discovery, approval, training and field results are future work. Aston vacancy 1155-26 · Aston listing R260050 · Public advert with candidate criteria

Turning the prototype into a pilot

Discovery workshop. Invite an operator, a maintenance representative, a buyer, a distributor or integrator and a service lead. Ask the operator to reconstruct the most recent interruption and the buyer to explain who authorised the response and paid for it. Check the account against a work order if available. Ask the team which single responsibility they would transfer first. Close by recording the assumption the team disagrees about, a test, an owner and a date.

Future product design review. Before releasing a revised table design, ask how a service colleague would identify the installed asset, obtain the correct parts and record completion. Ask the customer how they would report an interruption. Confirm who can approve the service instructions. Record unresolved dependencies before an offer goes to sales.

Two versions of the proposition. A buyer-facing pilot invitation could read, “Choose one transfer point you already own. Estimate what interruptions cost under your current arrangements and see how much time a recurring support service would need to recover to cover its fee. Agree the scope and review the records together before renewal.” A technical handover would identify the asset, site contact, included visits and parts, reporting route, incident definitions, exclusions and sign-off. The pilot must establish the quantities, prices, response windows and acceptance criteria.

Handover and development. Sales explains an offer and its exclusions to a colleague playing the buyer. A service colleague then estimates the resources for one visit. Finance reconciles the estimate with the model and records any correction. The next internal owner repeats the exercise on another case. For professional development, the associate can shadow an engineer, practise interviewing with an academic supervisor and review a pricing proposal with finance. Keep one example of a resulting change for each activity.

Research and publication. A question for a field report is whether a planned service changes interruption time per scheduled operating hour enough to cover the incremental delivery cost. Agree the event definition, denominator, observation periods, data access and attribution rules in advance. Record operating mix, exposure and missing logs for both periods. Pair the comparison with interviews about acceptance and incentives. A single-site before/after study can suggest what to test next. Discuss alternative explanations and the limits on generalisation in any paper.

Site and governance questions. Who authorises an observation and a pause if the activity becomes unsafe? Can workers on different shifts and people with access needs participate? Who can review or withdraw interview material before publication? Collect the minimum information needed and agree how dissenting feedback is reported. Track parts replaced, site visits and travel distance before estimating environmental effects. Use site-approved procedures and qualified personnel for any physical work.

Experience adapting theory or tools to commercial practice Essential Explain why the blueprint, costing or pilot method was adapted. Give a verified example of commercial implementation from professional experience.
Analytical problem-solving across technical and commercial decisions Essential Demonstrate one change in Economics and explain its effects on the customer and provider. Identify which assumption to validate first.
Stakeholder engagement, interdisciplinary collaboration and communication at multiple levels Essential Use the buyer and technical versions above. Add an example of working with stakeholders who had different priorities.
Managing complex projects, coordinating responsibilities and contributing independently and in a team Essential Walk through a pilot decision, who owns it and the evidence required. Explain individual responsibilities and collaborators' contributions on a real project.
Relevant postgraduate qualification or equivalent experience in engineering management, service design, operations, innovation, marketing or a related field Desirable Document the qualification or relevant experience on the application.
Manufacturing or industrial experience Desirable Provide an accurate account of direct industry exposure and responsibilities. A desk-based case demonstrates preparation.
Customer research, data analysis and commercially informed strategy Desirable Demonstrate the research plan and model, then corroborate research conducted or commercial decisions influenced with real examples.
Understanding of servitization, product–service systems or business-model innovation Desirable Explain how moving an activity from customer to supplier changes value, cost, capability and risk for both parties.
